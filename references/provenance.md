# Provenance: is this data real?

A "realistic" dataset and a company's actual operating records are different
things, and a report must not blur them. Classify every dataset, show the
evidence, and give the student wording they can paste into their report.

## The classes

| Class | What it means | Typical signs | What the student may claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **actual** | Records or statistics produced by real activity and published by the organisation that generated them, or by an official body | Named publisher, collection method described, period stated, often a paper or a regulator behind it | Findings describe that real organisation, market or period |
| **anonymized** | Real records with identifiers removed, masked, hashed or transformed | "Anonymised", "de-identified", hashed IDs, company name withheld, values scaled or PCA-transformed | Findings describe real behaviour; the organisation cannot be named and some variables cannot be interpreted |
| **simulated** | Produced by a model of a process, or designed by a vendor as a demonstration of a fictional company | "Fictional company", "sample data", "generated by a simulator", "based on" a real process | Findings demonstrate a method. They are not evidence about any real company |
| **synthetic** | Generated by an algorithm or by hand to look plausible, with or without a real dataset behind it | "Synthetic", "generated", "for learning purposes", suspiciously clean, round numbers, perfectly balanced groups | Same as simulated: method demonstration only |
| **unverified** | Nothing on the source page says where the data came from | No publisher beyond an uploader's username, no method, no period | Treat as synthetic for disclosure, and say the origin is unknown |

`simulated` and `synthetic` are both "not real". The distinction is worth
keeping because a simulator calibrated on real logs is closer to reality than a
random generator, but neither supports a claim about a real business.

A dataset can be real and still unrepresentative: one bank in one country in
2008, two hotels, one hospital network. That goes under limitations and bias,
not under provenance.

## How to decide

Work down this list and stop at the first that settles it. Quote the sentence
that settles it in `--provenance-note`.

1. **The publisher's own description.** The landing page, the README, the data
   dictionary, the file's documentation.
2. **The paper or report it came with.** UCI, Zenodo, Dataverse and data
   journals link one. The methods section says how the data was produced.
3. **The original source behind a mirror.** Community uploads usually copy
   something. Find the original and classify that. A mirror can also be an
   altered or partial copy, so say which one was downloaded.
4. **The catalog's discussion thread.** Useful as a pointer, not as proof.
5. **Nothing found.** Class is `unverified`. Do not guess upward.

Signs in the file itself that argue against "real" (worth noting, never
conclusive alone): no dates anywhere, no missing values at all, every category
almost exactly equal in size, IDs that are a plain running number, values that
are all round, a row count that is exactly 1,000 or 10,000.

## Well-known traps

These are widely used in tutorials and widely mistaken for real company data.
The notes are from general knowledge: **confirm on the source page before
relying on them**, and cite the page, not this file.

| Dataset as usually named | What it is generally understood to be |
|---|---|
| IBM "Telco Customer Churn" (`WA_Fn-UseC_-Telco-Customer-Churn.csv`, about 7,000 customers) | An IBM sample dataset describing a fictional telecom company. Simulated. It has no dates |
| IBM "HR Analytics Employee Attrition & Performance" | A fictional dataset created by IBM data scientists. Synthetic |
| Tableau "Sample - Superstore" | A fictional retailer shipped as product sample data |
| Microsoft AdventureWorks, Northwind, Contoso, Wide World Importers | Fictional companies built to demonstrate database products |
| "Mall Customers" segmentation data | Created for teaching; no documented real source |
| "Churn_Modelling.csv" bank churn (10,000 customers) | No documented source; treat as unverified |
| PaySim mobile money fraud | Output of a simulator calibrated on real mobile-money logs. Simulated |
| AI4I 2020 Predictive Maintenance (UCI) | Described by its authors as synthetic |
| NASA C-MAPSS turbofan degradation | Engine simulation output. Simulated |
| "Medical Cost Personal" (`insurance.csv`) | Teaching data from a textbook, simulated from demographic statistics |
| Practice datasets from training providers | Often fictional by design; each page says so |

Datasets generally understood to be real, for contrast (still verify): Olist
Brazilian E-Commerce (anonymized commercial orders), UCI Online Retail and
Online Retail II (a UK online retailer's transactions), UCI Bank Marketing (a
Portuguese bank's campaigns), UCI Default of Credit Card Clients (Taiwan), the
ULB credit card fraud data (real transactions, PCA-transformed), Instacart
orders (anonymized), NYC TLC trip records, CFPB complaints, UCI Diabetes 130-US
Hospitals, the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset, the hotel booking
demand data published in *Data in Brief*.

## When the best-known dataset is not real

This happens constantly, and it is not a reason to abandon the topic. The
options, in order of preference:

1. **Find real records and derive the measure.** Churn, attrition, delay,
   default and repeat purchase can often be computed from transaction, order or
   event data that is real.
2. **Use the fictional dataset as a practice-grade primary and anchor the
   business case in real aggregates.** Regulator statistics and company reports
   supply real rates, costs and trends. The report then says clearly which parts
   rest on which.
3. **Narrow the topic to where real data exists** - a neighbouring industry, a
   different country, an aggregate-level question.

Tell the user which option you chose and what it costs them: a method
demonstration on fictional data is a legitimate internship project, but its
recommendations are illustrative, not evidence about a real company.

## Disclosure wording

Put a statement like one of these in `README.md` and
`02_data_collection_report.md`, adapted to the facts. The student copies it into
their report.

**Actual / anonymized**

> This project uses the *[name]* dataset published by *[publisher]* ([link],
> retrieved [date]). It contains real *[what]* from *[who / where]* covering
> *[period]*, with *[identifiers]* anonymized by the publisher. Findings
> describe this population and period and should not be generalised beyond it
> without further evidence.

**Simulated / synthetic**

> This project uses the *[name]* dataset ([link], retrieved [date]). According
> to its publisher it is *[fictional sample data / simulator output / synthetic
> data]* and does not contain the records of any real organisation. The analysis
> demonstrates the analytical method; its numerical findings and recommendations
> are illustrative and are not evidence about a real company.

**Unverified**

> The origin of the *[name]* dataset ([link], retrieved [date]) is not
> documented by its uploader, and it could not be traced to an original source.
> It is treated here as synthetic. Results demonstrate method only.

**Mixed sources**

> Row-level analysis uses *[dataset A]* (*[class]*). Industry context and
> benchmark figures come from *[source B]* (*actual, aggregate*). The two are
> not joined; *[B]* is used only to *[calibrate assumptions / compare rates]*.

## Data the user asks you to generate

Only when explicitly requested. Then:

- Save under `data/synthetic/` with `SYNTHETIC` in every filename.
- Record in the report how it was generated, the seed, and which real
  statistics (with sources) the generator was calibrated on, if any.
- Never mix generated rows into a real table.
- Keep the simulated/synthetic disclosure in every document that uses it.
